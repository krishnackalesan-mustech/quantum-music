I The concept

What does a quantum computer sound like?

For a hundred years quantum mechanics has told us something stubborn and strange about reality: at its smallest scale, the universe doesn't make decisions until it's asked. A particle isn't here or there - it's both, until it's measured. This is not a metaphor. It's the deepest, best-tested theory of the physical world we have, and it sits behind every transistor, every laser, every MRI scan.

Quantum computers are the first machines built to think in this language directly. They don't process ones and zeros so much as probabilities - vast clouds of possibility that collapse into definite answers only at the moment of measurement. They are very nearly the strangest engineering project humans have ever undertaken, and they will reshape cryptography, chemistry, drug discovery and machine learning over the coming decade.

This project asks a question those fields don't usually consider: if a quantum computer's natural language is probability, and music's natural language is the unfolding of events through time, what happens when you let the first speak through the second?

The answer, I've found, is a kind of music no human or conventional algorithm could compose. It is not random - randomness sounds like noise. It is structured uncertainty: notes that emerge from the actual mathematics of quantum measurement, biased by the same correlations physicists call entanglement, played in real time through a software synthesiser and shaped, on stage, by a performer.

i.

Superposition

A qubit doesn't pick a value until measured. Until then, it holds every possibility at once - a coin spinning in the air, not a coin on the table.

ii.

Measurement

Observing the qubit collapses its possibilities into one definite outcome. The act of looking is what forces the universe to choose.

iii.

Entanglement

Two qubits can be linked so completely that their measurements stay correlated forever. This is the structure that turns noise into music.

Each glowing cluster is one quantum measurement. Eight corners - eight possible outcomes - but the universe never lights all of them.

"Until you measure, every outcome is real at once. The act of looking is what forces the universe to choose."

The piece you're about to hear in the next section is built on three qubits - eight possible measurement outcomes - mapped onto the notes of a musical scale. With entanglement on, only two of the eight outcomes are possible. With it off, all eight occur with equal probability. You can hear the difference. That is what makes this music quantum.

§ III How it's built

The architecture.

Five layers, in series. A quantum circuit generates a measurement; the measurement is mapped to a musical scale; the resulting MIDI is sent to a digital audio workstation; the same data is broadcast over OSC to a real-time visual engine; the performer shapes the result live with a hardware controller.

The stack.

Quantum
Qiskit

IBM's open-source quantum framework. Constructs and simulates the 3-qubit circuit that drives every note. The same code can be run on real IBM hardware via the cloud.

Audio
Mido + Logic Pro

MIDI is sent over a virtual IAC bus into Logic Pro, where any software instrument can voice the measurements. The system is timbre-agnostic - choose any sound.

Visual
TouchDesigner

OSC carries the same quantum data into a node-based visual engine. Particles spawn at cube corners corresponding to the measurement bits, coloured by pitch.

Performance
Akai MPK Mini

A pad and knob controller for live modulation. Switch scales, swap circuit modes, modulate tempo and density during performance.

Live, from the terminal.

The Python script is intentionally short - the project's value lies in what it routes, not in cleverness of code. Below: the source on top and the live terminal at the bottom. Each pad press on the MPK Mini prints a line - scale → Blues, mode → GHZ, PANIC: all notes off - proving the loop is running, listening, and switching state in real time as the performer plays.

The core loop.

Stripped to its essentials, the engine of the whole system fits in twenty lines.

# quantum_live.py - the heart of the system
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from pythonosc.udp_client import SimpleUDPClient
import mido, time

SCALE = [60, 62, 64, 65, 67, 69, 71, 72]   # C major
simulator = AerSimulator()
port = mido.open_output('IAC Driver Bus 1')
osc  = SimpleUDPClient("127.0.0.1", 9000)

while True:
    qc = QuantumCircuit(3)
    qc.h(0)                  # superposition
    qc.cx(0, 1)              # entangle 0 → 1
    qc.cx(1, 2)              # entangle 1 → 2 (GHZ state)
    qc.measure_all()

    binary = list(simulator.run(qc, shots=1).result().get_counts().keys())[0]
    note   = SCALE[int(binary, 2)]

    osc.send_message("/quantum/note", note)
    osc.send_message("/quantum/binary", binary)

    port.send(mido.Message('note_on', note=note, velocity=120))
    time.sleep(0.4)
    port.send(mido.Message('note_off', note=note, velocity=0))

Three modes of being quantum.

The performer can switch between three different quantum circuits live, each producing a distinct musical character:

  • GHZ mode - three qubits maximally entangled. Only two measurement outcomes are possible: 000 or 111. The result is starkly minimal - two notes, alternating, far apart in pitch.
  • BELL mode - two qubits entangled, one free. Four outcomes occur with equal probability. The most musical setting - paired notes that always rise or fall together.
  • FREE mode - three independent superpositions, no entanglement. All eight notes occur with equal probability. The least quantum, but the most melodically varied.

The mode itself becomes a compositional parameter. Switching from GHZ to FREE during a performance is the quantum equivalent of changing key - except what's changing isn't the harmony, it's how much quantum-ness is shaping the music in that moment.

§ IV Why this matters

Music technology meets the next computational paradigm.

The history of music technology is the history of new physical principles being borrowed into the studio. The valve gave us the electric guitar. The transistor gave us the synthesiser. Digital signal processing gave us sampling, plug-ins, the entire modern producer's toolkit. Each of these waves of innovation began far outside music - in defence labs, in computer science departments, in radio engineering - and arrived in our practice through the work of artists willing to misuse the new tools until they sang.

Quantum computing is the next of those waves, and it has barely begun to touch music. A handful of pioneering projects have explored the territory: Eduardo Reck Miranda's research on quantum computer music at the University of Plymouth has produced compositions that use real quantum hardware as a generative source [1]. Omar Costa Hamido's Quantum Synth proposes circuits as instruments in their own right [2]. The Quantum Music Playground from IBM Research re-imagines the qubit as a tile in a step sequencer [3]. These are early experiments in what will become a much larger field.

What's distinctive about this project, I hope, is not the use of quantum measurements - others have done that - but the integration. The quantum layer, the audio engine, the immersive visualisation, and the hardware controller all run as one continuous, performable system. The performer is not pressing a button to trigger a recording. They are playing a quantum process, with their hands, in real time.

"What's at stake here is not whether quantum computing makes interesting music. It clearly can. The question is what music will become when the strangeness of the physical world becomes part of the musician's vocabulary."

Within five years, quantum hardware will be substantially more accessible than it is today. Cloud APIs from IBM, Google, IonQ and others already let any musician with a laptop send a circuit to a real quantum processor and hear back a result in seconds. The technical barrier has fallen dramatically, even since this project started. What hasn't yet developed is the artistic vocabulary - the techniques, conventions, and aesthetic instincts that turn a new technology into a living musical practice. That vocabulary will be built by musicians, not physicists.

This project is one small contribution to that vocabulary. It demonstrates that quantum-driven music can be expressive, controllable, performable, and immediate - not just an academic curiosity, but a real instrument that you can stand behind and play.

References & further reading

  1. Eduardo Reck Miranda - Quantum Computer Music: Foundations, Methods and Advanced Concepts (Springer, 2022). The foundational text on the field; covers theory, history, and practical methods for composing with quantum circuits.
  2. Omar Costa Hamido - Quantum Synth and QAC Toolkit. A composer-researcher developing software that brings quantum circuit design directly into Max/MSP.
  3. James Weaver et al. - Quantum Music Playground (IBM Research). A web-based step sequencer where each row is a qubit, demonstrating quantum measurement as compositional material.
  4. IBM Quantum - Qiskit documentation and free cloud access to real quantum processors. qiskit.org
  5. Derivative - TouchDesigner reference and community. derivative.ca
  6. ISMIR & NIME - Music technology conferences (International Society for Music Information Retrieval; New Interfaces for Musical Expression) where quantum-driven systems have begun to appear as papers and performances.
§ V Reflection

How I worked.

This project came about because I was curious. During my placement, I attended a music technology event where I was first introduced to quantum computing in a creative context. The idea stayed with me. I began going to talks, visiting exhibitions across London, and learning Qiskit so I could work with quantum systems hands-on.

The work itself unfolded in three loops.

1. Getting to know the system.

I started by building simple quantum circuits and watching how they behaved. I plotted probability distributions and used the Bloch sphere as a mental model for how qubit states evolve. None of this was about music yet - it was about developing an intuition for a system whose rules are genuinely strange.

2. Learning to listen.

The next loop was about translation. I converted quantum measurements into MIDI and brought them into Logic Pro. At first the results sounded completely random, and that forced me to question what musical actually means in this context.

The shift came when I stopped treating the quantum part as a randomness generator and started treating it as a compositional tool. A simple superposition - three independent Hadamard gates - produces a flat, uniform distribution: that's not music, that's noise. But when I introduced entanglement, particularly GHZ states with their perfect correlations between qubits, the data became structured. Notes started to relate to each other. The output stopped sounding like a random number generator and started sounding like a system with internal logic.

3. Building the instrument.

The final loop was about integration and performance. I connected Python with TouchDesigner so the visuals could update in real time. I added the MPK Mini as a control surface. I worked on reducing latency until the relationship between gesture, sound, and image became perceptible to an audience - which is the threshold that separates a software demo from a musical instrument.

Each loop involved trial, failure, and adjustment, but together they shaped a system in which quantum behaviour can be experienced rather than just observed.

What I learned.

Four things, in roughly this order of importance:

  1. The interface is the project. Quantum mathematics is well-documented; music technology is well-documented; the bridge between them is where the real work lives. Decisions about what to surface - entanglement rather than just measurement; circuit-mode switching as a compositional control; pitch-driven colour as a visual link - are the substance of the project, not its implementation.
  2. Real-time matters more than physical authenticity. I chose Qiskit's local simulator over IBM's real hardware because the latency from cloud quantum processors makes live performance impossible. The physics is identical; the timing is not. This is a recurring tension in music technology - between fidelity and immediacy - and the answer for performance is almost always immediacy.
  3. The audience needs to see what they're hearing. A purely audible quantum-music system is fine for headphones. A live performance asks for a visual layer that doesn't just decorate the music but reveals its structure. Pitch-driven colour, particles spawning at probability-cube corners, the visible asymmetry between entangled and non-entangled modes - these were the elements that made the system legible as quantum to people who don't read papers about it.
  4. Music is already mathematics. This project reinforced something I think a lot of musicians intuit but rarely articulate: translating quantum data into music isn't unnatural. It's a shift in scale and perspective. Rhythm is the division of time; pitch is frequency; harmony is ratio. What quantum systems offer is a different kind of structure - probabilistic, non-classical, but still mathematical. The challenge isn't turning maths into music. It's choosing which mathematical relationships are meaningful enough to be heard.

Where it goes next.

Three directions, in increasing order of ambition:

  1. A multi-wall LED installation deployment, so the audience walks through the quantum state rather than watching it on a laptop screen.
  2. Full IBM Quantum hardware integration for non-real-time validation: pre-record measurement sequences from a real quantum processor and use them as the seed for compositions.
  3. Polyphony - multiple parallel circuits feeding multiple instruments, with entanglement between circuits so the harmonic relationships between voices are themselves quantum-correlated.

Each of these is additive. None requires rebuilding what's already been built. The system, as it stands, is a foundation - and a working live instrument that I plan to keep performing on.
